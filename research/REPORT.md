# Getting through a strong urge: what the research supports

Research report for the urge reset page. Written in plain English, for adults of any gender who want to stay faithful to a partner or to their own values.

**How to read this report**

- Every claim has a numbered source in the reference list at the end.
- **[VERIFY]** marks a claim I could not confirm from a primary source. Check it before it is shown to the public.
- Evidence strength is rated in plain words: **strong** (several studies or a meta-analysis), **moderate** (a few good studies), **weak** (one small study, or mixed results), **untested for sexual urges** (the evidence comes from food, alcohol or smoking).
- No study was invented. Where the evidence is thin, the report says so.

**The most important caveat, stated once:** almost all of the research on handling cravings in the moment was done on food, alcohol, cigarettes and drugs. Very little tested sexual urges directly. Applying it to sexual urges is a reasonable step, not a proven one.

## 1. How urges and cravings work

### 1.1 An urge starts as an intrusion and grows by being pictured

The best-supported account of craving is the Elaborated Intrusion (EI) theory of desire [1]. It describes two stages:

1. **Intrusion.** A thought about the wanted thing pops into the mind on its own. It is set off by something outside (a sight, a place, a phone), by the body (tiredness, tension), or by another thought. This first step is automatic. Nobody chooses it.
2. **Elaboration.** If the intrusion feels pleasant or promises relief, the mind begins to build on it: it calls up pictures, sounds and feelings of having the wanted thing. This step uses working memory, the small mental space we use to hold things in mind.

The pictures are the engine. In EI theory, sensory images are "especially important" because they simulate having the thing. They feel good for a moment, and then they sharpen the sense of what is missing, which makes the urge stronger [1]. This is why an urge can grow while a person is sitting still and doing nothing: the growing is happening in the imagination.

Two practical points follow, and the page is built on both:

- **The first thought is not a choice, and not a failure.** What a person does with it in the next seconds is where choice lives.
- **Working memory is limited.** If something else fills the mental space that imagery needs, the urge has less to feed on. This is the reasoning behind the visual games in section 2.1.

Evidence strength for EI theory: **strong** as a general account of craving (ten years of supporting studies are reviewed in [2]), **untested for sexual urges** in most of that work.

### 1.2 Rise, peak and fall: how long does an urge last?

Clinicians who teach "urge surfing" describe an urge as a wave: it rises, peaks and falls if it is not acted on. Many treatment websites give a figure of 15 to 30 minutes.

**This figure is a clinical rule of thumb, not a measured finding.** A search for a primary study that timed urges and found "20 to 30 minutes" turned up none. The sources that give the number are treatment centres and therapy directories, and those that say "studies show" do not name a study [3]. Reported ranges vary widely (3 to 10 minutes for food, 5 to 15 minutes or up to an hour for drugs), which is what one would expect of an estimate, not of a result [3]. **[VERIFY]** before stating any number of minutes as fact.

What can be said honestly:

- Urges change in strength over time, and a single urge does not last forever. This is consistent with the theory in 1.1: when elaboration stops, the urge loses its fuel.
- How long that takes differs between people and between moments.
- The page should say "urges rise and fall" and "this will pass", and should not say "it will be gone in X minutes".

The page's 5-minute rescue is therefore not based on urges ending at 5 minutes. It is based on the length of the tasks that were actually tested (3 minutes of a visual game, section 2.1) plus a short check and a plan.

### 1.3 Why pushing a thought away makes it louder

In a classic experiment, people told *not* to think of a white bear still thought of it about once a minute. When later allowed to think of it, they thought about it more than people who had never been asked to suppress it. This is called the rebound effect [4].

The same pattern has been shown for behaviour. Smokers asked to suppress thoughts of smoking for a week smoked less during that week, then smoked significantly more the week after than smokers in the comparison groups [5].

Caveats: later studies are mixed. At least one follow-up found that suppressing smoking thoughts did not raise the desire to smoke [5]. So the fair statement is "suppression often backfires", not "suppression always backfires".

One detail from the white bear work matters for the page: people who were given something else specific to think about did better than people who simply tried not to think [4]. Turning attention *toward* something works better than pushing a thought *away*.

Evidence strength: **moderate** (classic finding, mixed replications), **untested for sexual urges**.

## 2. What helps in the moment

### 2.1 A short visual task (the strongest fit for this page)

**The study.** Skorka-Brown, Andrade, Whalley and May (2015) followed 31 undergraduates for a week in their daily lives. Seven times a day, a text message asked them to report any craving and its strength. One group then played Tetris for 3 minutes and reported again; the other group only reported [6].

**The result.** Playing reduced craving strength by 13.9 percentage points on average (the authors describe it as cravings falling from about 70% to 56%). The effect held across drugs (alcohol, nicotine, caffeine), food and drink, and "activities", a category that included sex and gaming. It stayed consistent over the whole week; it did not wear off with repetition [6][7].

**Why it works, according to the authors.** A visual game needs the same visual working memory that craving imagery needs. It is hard to picture the wanted thing vividly while tracking and rotating shapes [6][7].

**Honest limits.**

- 31 participants, all students. This is a small study.
- The reduction was about one fifth. Cravings became weaker; they did not vanish.
- Sexual urges were part of a broad "activities" group. The study did not report an effect for sexual urges on their own. **[VERIFY]** against the full paper before claiming a specific number for sexual urges.
- The authors themselves called for testing in people with dependence.

**What this means for the page.** The game must be visual and spatial (shapes, colour, movement, position), must demand continuous attention, and should last about 3 minutes. Reading tasks and word games do not fit the mechanism. The page should say a game "can make the urge weaker", never "removes it".

Evidence strength: **moderate** for cravings in general (this field study plus earlier lab studies reviewed in [2]), **weak** for sexual urges specifically.

### 2.2 Urge surfing

Urge surfing means watching an urge with curiosity: noticing where it sits in the body, how it rises and shifts, and letting it be there without obeying it and without fighting it.

**The study.** Bowen and Marlatt (2009) gave college-student smokers either brief mindfulness instructions that included urge surfing, or no instructions, during exposure to cigarette cues [8].

**The result, which is often misreported.** The two groups did **not** differ in how strong their urges were. What differed was behaviour: over the following 7 days, the urge-surfing group smoked significantly fewer cigarettes. The link between feeling bad and feeling an urge was also weaker in that group [8]. Secondary sources give a figure of a 37% reduction by day seven; that number comes from a blog and should be treated as **[VERIFY]** [9].

**What this means for the page.** Urge surfing should be presented honestly: it may not make the urge smaller right now. It teaches that an urge can be present without being acted on. That is a different benefit from the visual game, and it is why the page offers the game for strong urges (where lowering intensity matters most) and surfing for milder ones (where there is room to practise).

**Related, and closer to the topic.** Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), which uses the same "allow it, don't obey it" stance, was tested for problematic pornography use in a randomised trial of 28 men. Viewing fell by 93% in the treated group against 21% in the waiting group [10]. This was 12 sessions with a therapist, not a self-help page; the sample was small and almost all from one religious community; and quality of life did not measurably improve. It supports the approach. It does not show that a web page achieves the same.

Evidence strength: **moderate** for changing behaviour, **weak** for reducing urge strength, **weak but direct** for sexual behaviour (through the ACT trial).

### 2.3 Slow breathing

Slow, paced breathing is widely recommended, and the evidence is weaker than its reputation.

- A pilot study on food craving found that slow paced breathing did **not** reduce current craving compared with faster breathing [11].
- In that paper's review of earlier work, one study of smokers suggested slow breathing briefly weakened the urge to smoke, and another found that an instruction to focus on breathing did not reduce food craving [11].
- A 2026 systematic review of 20 studies reported that breathing-based interventions gave small but statistically significant reductions in substance craving, including some single-session effects. Only the press release was available, so this is **[VERIFY]** [12].

**What this means for the page.** Use slow breathing as a steadying rhythm inside the wave exercise, and as something to do with the body. Do not present it as a proven way to reduce urges.

Evidence strength: **weak / mixed**, **untested for sexual urges**.

### 2.4 Short physical movement

A meta-analysis that pooled individual data from up to 17 studies found that a single short bout of physical activity reduced cigarette craving in smokers who had temporarily stopped. The effect was large, and every study pointed in the same direction, though the size varied a lot between studies [13]. A follow-up analysis found that moderate and vigorous activity helped most [13].

Limits: this is cigarette craving in abstaining smokers. Nobody has run the same test on sexual urges. The mechanism (a shift in body state and attention) is plausible for other urges, and movement is low-risk.

**What this means for the page.** Offer a 2-minute movement guide (stand up, walk, brisk steps, stretches) as the second round when an urge stays high.

Evidence strength: **strong** for cigarette craving, **untested for sexual urges**.

### 2.5 Leaving the situation

Self-control research describes five points at which a person can act on an impulse, from earliest to latest: choosing the situation, changing the situation, directing attention, changing how one thinks about it, and resisting directly. The earlier strategies are predicted to work better, because impulses grow with time and are easier to stop before they are strong [14].

Leaving the room, putting the phone in another room, or going where other people are, all act at the "situation" stage. They are the cheapest effective actions available.

Limits: [14] is a theoretical review with supporting studies, mostly in students and on study habits and eating. It is a framework, not a trial on sexual urges.

Evidence strength: **moderate** (strong theory, supporting field studies), **untested for sexual urges**.

### Summary table

| Tool | What it does | Evidence | Tested on sexual urges? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-minute visual game | Lowers craving strength by about a fifth | Moderate | Partly (inside a mixed group) |
| Urge surfing | Changes what people do, not how strong the urge feels | Moderate | Indirectly (ACT trial) |
| Slow breathing | Steadies; effect on craving is mixed | Weak | No |
| Short movement | Lowers cigarette craving a lot | Strong for smoking | No |
| Leaving the situation | Stops the impulse early | Moderate | No |

## 3. What helps over the long term

### 3.1 If-then plans

An if-then plan (researchers call it an "implementation intention") links a specific moment to a specific action: "If I am in bed with my phone after 22:00, then I put it on the far side of the room." The decision is made in advance, so that in the moment the action needs less willpower.

A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d = 0.65) [15]. Some sources add "over 8,000 participants"; that number appears in secondary write-ups and could not be confirmed from the publisher's record. **[VERIFY]**

**An important update.** A much larger follow-up analysis by the same research group (642 tests) found that when statistical corrections for publication bias are applied, the effect is smaller: d = 0.35 by one method and d = 0.15 by a stricter one. Only secondary reports of this follow-up were found, so the exact numbers are **[VERIFY]** [16].

**What this means for the page.** If-then plans are worth building in: they are free, quick and have a real effect. The page should describe them as "a small, reliable help", and should not quote the old d = 0.65 figure as if it were settled.

Good plans are specific (a place, a time, a feeling as the "if") and name an action that can be done in seconds (the "then").

Evidence strength: **strong** that there is an effect; its size is **smaller than first reported**. **Untested for sexual urges.**

### 3.2 Changing the environment

This is section 2.5 applied in advance. Because situation-level strategies are predicted to beat effortful resistance [14], the long-term version is to arrange life so that the hard moment comes less often: charging the phone outside the bedroom, not scrolling in bed, using content filters, not being alone with a screen late at night when that is the pattern.

Evidence strength: **moderate**, **untested for sexual urges**.

### 3.3 Knowing your triggers

EI theory says intrusions are set off by outside cues, body states and linked thoughts [1]. Knowing one's own cues makes if-then plans possible, because the cue becomes the "if". Bowen and Marlatt found that feeling bad and feeling urges are linked [8], which supports paying attention to mood states such as stress, loneliness, tiredness and boredom.

Note: the popular checklist of triggers (hungry, angry, lonely, tired) is a clinical teaching aid, not a research result. It is reasonable, and should be offered as "common triggers people report", not as a finding.

Evidence strength: **moderate** for the general principle.

### 3.4 Values: knowing why

ACT asks people to name what matters to them and to act toward it while uncomfortable feelings are present [10]. In the pornography trial, values work was part of a package that produced large changes in behaviour, so its separate contribution is unknown.

The page asks each person for one line on why this matters to them, and shows it back at the end of a rescue. This is a reasonable use of the idea. It is the person's own words, not a slogan from the page.

Evidence strength: **weak** on its own (never tested separately here).

### 3.5 After a slip, without shame

Relapse-prevention research describes the "abstinence violation effect": after breaking a self-set rule, people who respond with guilt, self-blame and a sense of having lost all control are more likely to slide from one slip into a full return to the old pattern. People who treat the slip as a specific mistake to learn from are more likely to return to their goal [17]. **[VERIFY]** the exact wording against Marlatt and Gordon's original work; the description here comes from summaries.

Experimental work on self-compassion points the same way: being kind to oneself after a lapse (in dieting) reduced further overeating, and self-compassion after failure raised the motivation to improve. These two findings are recalled from the literature (Adams and Leary, 2007; Breines and Chen, 2012) and were **not** confirmed in this search. **[VERIFY]**

**What this means for the page.** Shame is not a neutral motivator; it is a known route from one slip to many. The page must never show a "you failed" message. After a slip it should offer one question ("what was happening just before?") and one next step.

Evidence strength: **moderate** for the abstinence violation effect in substance use, **untested for sexual urges**.

## 4. Men and women

### 4.1 What is recognised

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 lists **compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (CSBD)**, code 6C72. It describes a persistent pattern of failing to control intense, repetitive sexual urges, resulting in repeated sexual behaviour that continues despite harm or despite giving little satisfaction, and that causes marked distress or impairment in important areas of life [18]. The WHO description speaks of a pattern over an extended period, giving six months or more as an example; **[VERIFY]** this wording on the WHO ICD-11 browser [18].

One point in the WHO text matters a great deal for this page: **distress that comes entirely from moral judgement or disapproval of one's own sexual urges is not enough for the diagnosis** [18]. **[VERIFY]** on the WHO browser. The diagnosis also remains debated among researchers [18].

### 4.2 How common it is

In a national probability sample of 4,633 people in Germany (the GeSiD survey), 4.9% of men and 3.0% of women reported experiences over their lifetime that matched the ICD-11 requirements. For the past 12 months the figures were 3.2% of men and 1.8% of women [19]. A correction to this paper was published later; its content was not seen. **[VERIFY]**

These are one country's figures and cannot be assumed for Rwanda or elsewhere. They do show two things: most people do not have this condition, and women are a substantial share of those who do.

### 4.3 How concerns differ

A study reported by PsyPost found that men were more likely than women to be worried about their sexual behaviour (11.8% against 3.4%). Men more often named solitary behaviour, such as pornography and masturbation. Women more often named partnered behaviour [20]. This is a secondary news report of one study; treat the percentages as **[VERIFY]**.

### 4.4 The research gap on women

A 2024 paper in the *Journal of Behavioral Addictions* states that current knowledge of CSBD comes mainly from non-clinical samples of heterosexual men, leaving large gaps about women: how common it is, what it looks like, what it comes with, and what stops women from seeking help. Earlier work by the same group suggested that stereotypes and lack of knowledge may make women less likely to seek help [21].

### 4.5 What this means for the page

- Use words that fit everyone. Do not assume the user is a man.
- Do not assume pornography is the problem. Examples of triggers and plans should cover both solitary situations (alone with a phone at night) and social ones (messaging someone, a meeting that could go somewhere, a colleague).
- Do not present sex differences as facts about any one person. They are averages from a small number of studies, mostly in Western countries.
- Be honest that the tools were mostly tested on mixed or male samples.

## 5. Permission-giving thoughts

### 5.1 The idea

In the cognitive therapy model of addiction developed by Aaron Beck and colleagues, there is a step between feeling an urge and acting on it. The step is a thought that gives permission: "just one won't hurt", "I deserve it". Therapists call these **permission-giving beliefs**. Treatment involves learning to notice them and to treat them as thoughts to examine, not as facts [22]. A clinical example from the same tradition is "I'll just have one drink tonight", appearing as soon as craving begins [22].

The model comes from substance use treatment. No study was found that tested the idea on sexual urges specifically. It is used here because the pattern of thought is the same, and because noticing and naming a thought is low-risk. **Untested for sexual urges.**

### 5.2 Five common ones, with a short true answer

The answers below are not arguments to win. They are the plain fact that the thought leaves out.

| The thought | What it leaves out | A short, true answer |
|---|---|---|
| "Just once won't matter." | Each time makes the next time easier. "Once" is how every pattern is built. | "Once is how it always starts. This one counts." |
| "Nobody will know." | One person will always know, and that person has to live with it. | "I will know. That is enough." |
| "I deserve this after today." | A hard day deserves real rest or comfort. This thought offers something that will cost more tomorrow. | "I deserve something good. This is not it." |
| "I'll start tomorrow." | Tomorrow's urge will say the same thing. The only time anyone can act is now. | "Tomorrow will say the same. I start now." |
| "I can't fight this." | It mistakes strength for permanence. Urges rise and fall, and the person has outlasted them before. Also, the task is not to fight; it is to wait and do something else. | "I don't have to fight it. I only have to let it pass." |

The last answer matters most. Section 1.3 shows that fighting a thought can feed it. The answer to "I can't fight this" is not "yes you can"; it is "you do not need to".

Twenty of these, in English and Kinyarwanda, are in `FALSE_THOUGHTS.md`.

### 5.3 A caution

These answers are meant for people who have themselves decided what they want (to be faithful, to keep a promise, to live by their values). They are reminders of the person's own choice. They are not rules imposed from outside, and the page must not use them to shame.

## 6. When self-help is not enough

A self-help page is suitable for an ordinary difficulty that many people have. A person should talk to a qualified professional (a doctor, psychologist or counsellor) if any of these are true:

- The behaviour has felt out of control for months, and repeated attempts to change it have not worked [18].
- It continues despite real harm: to a relationship, to work or study, to health, to money, or to safety [18].
- It continues although it brings little or no satisfaction [18].
- It has taken the central place in life, pushing out care for health, responsibilities or other interests [18].
- It comes with depression, heavy anxiety, or heavy use of alcohol or drugs. In the German survey, people who met the CSBD description were more likely to have had treatment for depression or another mental health problem in the past year [19].
- **There are thoughts of self-harm or suicide. This is urgent: get help today.**
- There is an urge toward anything that would harm another person, or that involves someone who cannot or does not consent, or a child. This needs professional help now, before anything happens.

Also worth saying on the page: strong distress that comes mainly from a clash between one's behaviour and one's moral or religious values is real suffering, and is common. Research calls this "moral incongruence" and finds it strongly linked to people describing themselves as addicted, even when their behaviour is not unusual [23]. A counsellor, or a trusted faith leader who does not shame, can help with this. The answer to that kind of distress is support, not harsher self-judgement.

### Help in Rwanda

The Rwanda Psychological Society's FAQ tells people in a crisis to contact RBC on 114 or go to a hospital [25]. ## 7. What the page must never claim

1. **That an urge will disappear, or be gone in a set number of minutes.** The tested effect is a reduction of about a fifth [6]. The honest promise is "this can help you get through it".
2. **That it is treatment or therapy.** It is a self-help tool.
3. **That it is "proven" for sexual urges.** Most evidence is from other cravings. The page should say so.
4. **That the user has an addiction or a disorder.** The page cannot diagnose. Most people who struggle with urges do not have CSBD [19], and moral distress alone is not a disorder [18].
5. **That sexual desire is bad, dirty or shameful.** Desire is ordinary. The page helps people act on their own values; it does not judge the feeling.
6. **That a slip means failure.** See 3.5. No streaks, no counters, no "you broke it" messages.
7. **That willpower is the answer.** The evidence favours attention, planning and changing the situation over trying harder [14].
8. **That the reminders ("truth lines") are science.** They are values. They belong in a clearly separate, labelled place.
9. **Anything about the brain that the studies here do not support.** No "rewiring", "dopamine reset" or "90-day reboot" language. None of the sources in this report supports those claims.
10. **That men and women are fundamentally different in this.** The differences found are averages from limited research [20][21].
11. **A helpline number that has not been checked.**
